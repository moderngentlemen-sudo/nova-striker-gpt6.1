Nova Striker — Fresh-Start Development Brief
I want to approach Nova Striker from the beginning as an original, fully considered game-development project. Help me reconstruct its creative and technical foundations while preserving the core story, character identities, visual direction, and gameplay ambitions below.
Act as a collaborative game director, gameplay designer, narrative designer, technical lead, and production planner. Challenge weak assumptions, explain meaningful tradeoffs, and help turn the concept into a coherent, enjoyable, realistically producible game—not merely an expanding feature list.
1. Scope boundary: a separate fresh-start exercise
This is an isolated, hypothetical fresh-start track. It does not replace, cancel, reset, or otherwise affect the existing Nova Striker project or any current work.
Treat “from the beginning” as the planning premise of this conversation, not permission to modify an existing implementation. Do not reset repositories, alter branches, overwrite documents or assets, change deployments, or reinterpret the status of ongoing work. Do not record this exercise as a project-wide decision to restart. Previous authorizations from other conversations do not authorize changes here.
For this exercise, assume we have the creative foundations described below but no implementation we are required to retain. Existing code, architecture, prototypes, and production workflows are not binding. Reusing any actual project material would require a separate, explicit decision.
Begin with planning and design. Any later implementation must be explicitly authorized and isolated from the active project. Do not claim to have inspected previous conversations, source files, or approved artwork unless they are actually available to you.
2. Project identity and creative ambition
Nova Striker is an original science-fiction action platformer, centered on expressive movement, satisfying combat, distinct playable characters, and cooperative teamwork. Use a side-scrolling action-platformer foundation; evaluate a 2.5D presentation using 3D characters and environments without assuming unrestricted 3D movement.
The ambition is a polished, responsive, visually distinctive game with depth and replayability. Strong game feel, legible encounters, and a memorable identity matter more than raw feature count.
Relevant inspiration includes Mega Man X for movement, action-platforming, and earned abilities; Overwatch for differentiated silhouettes, character appeal, and polished stylized presentation; and Invincible for bold shapes, clear color language, and an accessible graphic sensibility. These are reference points, not permission to reproduce recognizable characters, costumes, stories, assets, or signature material.
The visual direction should be premium, colorful, streamlined science fiction—not photorealism, generic military realism, or an accumulation of tiny mechanical details. The world can be beautiful and aspirational while supporting danger, loss, and serious emotional stakes.
3. Story foundation and central relationship
The setting is an advanced civilization with sophisticated energy systems, biomechanical technology, advanced transit, artificial environments, and memory-related technology. Near the Eclipse Core, the setting permits reality-bending phenomena. Establish understandable rules and consequences rather than using advanced technology to explain anything without limits.
Nova and Echo are both male. They are close friends who enter the Academy as accepted Striker candidates. They want to serve, protect others, achieve greatness, and preserve their friendship. Their relationship should combine affection, rivalry, contrasting instincts, and genuine dependence on one another.
Their future team placements and eventual Striker 0 or Striker 1 assignments are initially unknown. Do not silently assign them permanent ranks or teams. Whether they ultimately serve together or on different teams is an important story decision that remains open.
Nova has lost someone important. That experience drives an intense sense of responsibility and a protective, cautious, preventative approach. He prefers to keep danger at range and stop threats before they reach teammates or civilians. His strength is protection; his potential flaw is overprotection, including difficulty accepting other people’s autonomy and necessary risks. Do not invent the lost person’s identity or the circumstances of the loss as established canon.
Echo prefers close combat and is more willing to put himself in danger. He believes enduring hits and hardship can build discipline: he brings danger to himself rather than keeping it at a distance. Preserve that belief as characterization without making deliberately taking avoidable damage the only effective way to play him. His courage should have emotional substance, not become shallow recklessness.
Central themes include identity, memory, loss, discovery, preservation, responsibility, ambition, friendship, and the distinction between protecting people and controlling them. Propose the larger conflict, antagonistic forces, and character arcs without presenting new plot inventions as preexisting canon.
4. Academy, Strike Teams, and Strike Suits
The principal ensemble contains eight characters, organized into two four-person operational units: Strike Team 1 and Strike Team 2.
Each team has one Tank, one Striker 0, one Striker 1, and one Support. Striker 1 is the team leader; Striker 0 is second-in-command. Do not reverse these ranks or confuse them with the numbered teams. Leadership status should not automatically translate into objectively stronger gameplay statistics.
The two Strikers within a team have a dynamic analogous to two drivers on the same racing team: they compete for distinction while depending on each other to accomplish a shared objective. Make that cooperation and competition meaningful without turning the relationship into hostility by default.
Nova and Echo occupy the Striker track. The remaining principal characters require development. Their identities, personalities, combat approaches, suit abilities, and team assignments should be proposed rather than falsely described as settled. Eight principal story characters do not automatically mean eight fully playable launch characters; distinguish the narrative ensemble from the production roster.
Candidates who earn placement on either Strike Team receive the opportunity, upon graduation, to travel to Ember Works and create a personal Strike Suit. A Strike Suit combines specialized combat armor with an exoskeleton. It is individually built for its wearer, not a generic uniform, and grants a unique signature ability alongside protection and performance enhancement.
Keep three identity layers distinct: the character’s team role, their personal fighting style, and their individual Strike Suit ability. Guardian-earned powers form another progression layer; they must not erase the significance of a character’s personal suit. Do not interpret one signature suit ability as a limit of one combat action.
Use World Codex, not “World Bible,” for the central setting and continuity document.
5. Nova’s retained design and gameplay identity
Nova’s direction is soldier/military-inspired Sentinel, not hunter. Preserve the disciplined, protective, combat-ready identity. Do not import Echo’s pursuit aesthetic into Nova or revert Nova to an abandoned hunter direction.
The retained visual baseline is male, athletic, streamlined armor with a recognizable blue-and-white direction and warm gold/orange energy accents. Preserve an integrated forearm-weapon concept, functional articulation, and both an unhelmeted presentation and a full tactical helmet configuration. Exact color values, panel details, and production dimensions remain subject to design and reference review.
His silhouette should suggest mobility, precision, readiness, and protection—not a walking tank. Armor must support crouching, sliding, aiming, wall movement, and expressive poses without bulky intersections or unreadable masses.
Mechanically, Nova should favor ranged pressure, charged attacks, interception, controlled positioning, and protection that still feels active and aggressive. He must remain enjoyable in solo play rather than depending on teammates to make his kit relevant.
Retain Bulwark Pulse as an established ability direction, together with tactical helmet-enabled actions and an ultimate or heightened-combat-state concept. Define their exact behavior, costs, timing, visual language, and relationships as design work; do not fabricate previously approved specifications. Avoid giving him several differently named abilities that perform essentially the same defensive function.
His arc should concern how he protects others, not simply whether he becomes more powerful. Connect his suit and gameplay identity to his personality without making the narrative flaw an unavoidable frustration for the player.
6. Echo’s retained design and gameplay identity
Echo’s approved visual baseline is Pursuit Protocol, Variation 02 / field-ready configuration. Preserve his distinct pursuit-oriented, close-combat identity rather than turning him into a recolored Nova.
His retained design includes streamlined, masculine athletic armor; a white-and-black palette with deep yellow-orange emissive accents; an intact nano-adaptive scarf integrated with a protective collar; and a utility belt. The scarf is not a torn cape. Do not restore discarded wings or replace the approved scarf direction with earlier cape concepts.
Preserve a bare-face unhelmeted mode, a survival/tactical mask configuration that covers the ears, and a full helmet with a semi-transparent amber visor that allows his face to remain visible. The rendering reference is polished, stylized, Overwatch-like cinematic quality, adapted to the game’s own visual language and gameplay camera.
His equipment includes dual gauntlet multi-tools that can combine into a staff-like weapon with rifle functionality, with an appropriate back-mounted arrangement. Explore practical blade and short-, medium-, and long-range functions without producing an unlimited arsenal or obscuring his close-combat specialty.
The nano-scarf can contribute to clearly differentiated combat modes or move variations. Establish a small, understandable system rather than turning every animation into an expensive cloth or transformation effect.
Echo should emphasize pursuit, close-range pressure, melee combinations, aerial engagement, counters, and deliberate risk. His tools can provide ranged options, but they should not make Nova’s ranged identity redundant. Both protagonists need skill expression and survivability without converging on the same playstyle.
The written descriptions in this brief preserve direction, not exact image geometry. Request actual approved reference images before claiming production-accurate visual matching.
7. Regions, Guardians, and campaign framework
Preserve the following six regions and associated Guardian identities:
Region	Guardian	Core identity to retain
Skyport	Aegis	Defense, protection, and counterplay
Ember Works	Cinder	Heat, industry, and rushdown pressure
Verdant Vault	Mycel	Biological growth and battlefield control
Cryo Relay	Rime	Crystalline forms, cold, and control
Storm Spire	Tempest	Aerial movement and electrical forces
Eclipse Core	Null	Spatial distortion, gravity, and clones


Use the retained 18-act campaign framework—six regions with three acts each—as the full-game planning baseline, not the required size of the first prototype.
These regions should feel like parts of a functioning civilization rather than six interchangeable themed arenas. Develop their social purpose, environmental identity, relationship to the wider world, and consequences when systems fail.
Give each Guardian a recognizable silhouette, behavior language, encounter identity, and meaningful relationship to its region. Preserve Guardian-earned abilities as a progression foundation. Whether Guardians are antagonists, compromised protectors, conflicted systems, or something else requires narrative development; do not assume every Guardian is simply evil.
Propose campaign order, revisitation, optional routes, and the relationship between Academy progression, graduation, suit creation, and the wider crisis. Do not force a story chronology that contradicts the suit-creation foundation merely to fit a level list.
8. Core movement and combat
Preserve the movement vocabulary of running and backpedaling, crouching, powersliding, jumping and double jumping, wall clinging/sliding/jumping, multidirectional dashing, and Velocity Break. Define Velocity Break precisely before implementation rather than assuming its name establishes an agreed mechanic.
Preserve 360-degree aiming, standard and charged ranged attacks, grounded and aerial melee chains, parrying and perfect parrying, character-specific suit actions, Guardian abilities, and ultimate or heightened-combat states.
Depth should come from the interactions between movement, timing, positioning, enemies, and character choices—not simply from adding more buttons. Explain input priorities, animation cancellation rules, recovery windows, cooldowns, resource costs, and which systems actually belong in the first prototype.
Pay particular attention to controller-first ergonomics. Explain how aiming, jumping, firing, melee, and dashing remain accessible during fast action. Design keyboard-and-mouse and touch adaptations as deliberate input schemes rather than treating touch as a crowded overlay of controller buttons.
Give attacks readable startup, impact, and recovery. Establish a consistent language for ordinary threats, parryable attacks, unblockable attacks, armor, stagger, and invulnerability. Aim assistance, lock-on, directional selection, and manual aim should be explicit choices with clear purposes, not overlapping systems added by habit.
Build movement and combat together: dashes should affect engagement choices, air control should support intentional combat, and defensive actions should create opportunities rather than merely interrupt play.
Retain space for expressive weapons and unlocks, but do not carry forward an arbitrary weapon count or a legacy prototype’s limitations. Every addition should earn its complexity through a distinct tactical purpose.
9. Solo play and four-player cooperation
The game must be designed to support one to four simultaneous onscreen players. Do not reduce the design to the original two-protagonist pairing or assume that four-player support can be added after the rest of the game is finished.
Nova and Echo remain central characters, but define how additional playable characters participate. Separate the story cast, the prototype’s controllable test roles, the first playable roster, and the eventual roster.
Preserve downed/revive interactions and cooperative Sync/Synergy actions. Define how cooperation rewards coordination without making normal combat feel incomplete when playing alone.
Plan the shared-screen camera, player spread limits, offscreen recovery, vertical separation, boss framing, and foreground/background readability. Address drop-in/drop-out, controller assignment, reconnection, menu ownership, checkpoints, defeat and retry rules, and reward distribution.
Difficulty must scale through encounter composition, behavior, objectives, pressure, and available space—not only larger health bars. Tank and Support identities should be viable and enjoyable without making a particular team composition mandatory.
The main campaign must remain completable solo. Optional cooperative splits or simultaneous tasks should have a solo-safe alternative or be explicitly optional. Decide whether solo play uses one selected character, switching, or AI partners; do not assume all three must ship.
Treat local/shared-screen cooperation as the baseline design problem. Evaluate online cooperation separately. Four onscreen players does not, by itself, establish networking, cross-play, rollback, or cross-progression requirements. Identify their implications before recommending a commitment.
Four-player readability should influence enemy density, particle effects, damage feedback, interface layout, and character silhouettes from the first combat tests.
10. Level design, progression, and replayability
Develop a repeatable moment-to-moment loop and a broader campaign loop. Explain what the player does, what creates tension, what they learn, what they earn, and why they want another attempt or another act.
Build level variety through different layouts, traversal demands, hazards, encounter structures, and objectives—not only different textures. Consider horizontal action runs, vertical climbs, moving infrastructure, exploration branches, defensive encounters, pursuits, and set pieces as possibilities, not a mandatory checklist.
Use checkpoints, optional routes, secrets, elite encounters, and Guardian fights with deliberate pacing. Explain how the 18-act framework avoids repeating the same sequence six times. New abilities should create meaningful opportunities for exploration and combat without making early regions frustrating or requiring aimless backtracking.
Design progression across movement mastery, character-specific upgrades, weapons or tools, Guardian powers, and optional build choices. Keep the unique Strike Suit identity legible beneath these systems. Avoid adding overlapping skill trees, currencies, and upgrade layers without a clear need.
Recommend how challenge modes, replay scoring, difficulty options, alternate routes, or post-campaign content could increase longevity. Separate the strongest launch features from additions that can wait. Do not use grinding or inflated numerical progression as substitutes for encounter quality.
11. Visual production, animation, audio, and accessibility
Use Unity for gameplay development and Blender for character/environment modeling, rigging, and animation production as the working pipeline. A different foundation may be proposed with a specific justification, but do not silently replace this direction.
Start with functional blockouts and readable placeholder effects. The game should prove movement, combat, cooperation, camera behavior, and encounter readability before expensive final-asset production.
Characters need production-aware designs that support the full movement and combat vocabulary. Address joint clearance, weapon sockets, aiming poses, helmet variants, transformations, animation layering, and the distinction between gameplay animation and promotional cinematic animation. Keep both protagonists in one coherent visual family while retaining different silhouettes and motion personalities.
Plan a modular environment kit and a restrained effects language. Important cues must remain readable when four characters, enemies, projectiles, and hazards overlap.
Audio should clarify timing and state through weapon identities, movement feedback, parries, damage, Guardian actions, and Sync cues. Accessibility should include remapping, appropriate assistance options, adjustable visual intensity, alternatives to color-only information, and readable interfaces. Recommend settings that preserve the game’s identity without treating accessibility as an afterthought.
Do not describe concept art as a finished game-ready asset or a generated mesh as a validated rigged character.
12. Platforms, performance, and architecture
The long-term target platforms are Windows, macOS, Linux, Steam Deck, Nintendo Switch, Nintendo Switch 2, Xbox One, Xbox Series S/X, PlayStation 4, PlayStation 5, Android, iOS, and iPadOS.
Treat this as a broad compatibility ambition, not proof that every platform can or should launch simultaneously. Recommend a sensible lead development platform and staged porting strategy. Explain the implications of older consoles, handheld performance, mobile thermal limits, different input methods, and four-player screen density.
Propose measurable frame-rate and frame-time targets and scalable quality tiers. Prioritize responsiveness and stable delivery over effects that undermine play. Do not promise universal performance without profiling representative builds on representative hardware.
Before implementation decisions that depend on current facts, verify engine support, console access requirements, rendering compatibility, platform rules, SDK availability, and licensing from current official sources. Do not assume that choosing Unity automatically provides access to every platform or service.
Recommend the smallest maintainable architecture that supports the intended game: character control, input ownership, combat, abilities, enemies, encounters, camera, progression, saves, content definitions, interface, and testing. Explain responsibilities and integration boundaries rather than prescribing excessive abstractions.
Separate gameplay rules from presentation where useful, but do not build speculative networking, commerce, or live-service infrastructure before the core game requires it.
13. Commercial model and scope discipline
The project should be capable of supporting cosmetics, microtransactions, and downloadable content. This is a commercial capability to plan for, not permission to integrate real payments now or assume any particular revenue outcome.
Compare appropriate models, such as premium purchase with optional cosmetics and expansions versus alternatives. Use fair, non-pay-to-win monetization as the default recommendation, and distinguish proposed commercial principles from previously established game canon.
Do not make the core combat loop dependent on purchases, paid recovery, or artificial inconvenience. Evaluate the production and operational burden of cosmetics, storefronts, entitlements, downloadable characters, and additional campaigns before recommending them.
Keep prototype scope separate from launch scope and longer-term ambition. A system is not automatically a launch requirement because it appears in this brief. Identify the smallest version that still demonstrates what is distinctive about Nova Striker.
Do not let monetization planning, platform breadth, or an eight-character ensemble displace the immediate need to prove that movement, combat, and cooperation are fun.
14. Development method and validation
Organize the work into stages with explicit deliverables and exit criteria: foundation design, movement/combat prototype, cooperative systems testing, representative vertical slice, production planning, full content production, and release validation.
The first prototype should answer specific questions about responsiveness, character distinction, camera behavior, encounter design, and solo/co-op viability. Explain which temporary shortcuts are acceptable and which would invalidate the experiment.
Use simple test rooms and blockout encounters to validate individual mechanics before building full acts. Include a four-player stress test early enough to expose structural problems, without requiring four finished production characters immediately.
For every stage, distinguish design decisions, code written, static checks, engine compilation, runtime tests, and hardware validation. Do not report a feature as working merely because its files exist or a non-engine check passed. Identify what needs human playtesting and what can be tested automatically.
When implementation is authorized, work in bounded, reviewable passes. Report completed changes, evidence, limitations, and the next dependency. Do not claim to continue working unattended or promise a later result without an actual supported mechanism.
Keep the existing project untouched throughout this separate track.
